THE PROBLEM & OUR APPROACH

Why the message
needs a reserve.

Imagine a maintenance team at a fixed receiving point waiting for an update that stops halfway through. Reusing suitable wiring could reduce the need for another cable route, but uneven delivery still has to be managed. This is a proposed use, not a mine-tested service.

THE PURPOSE

Help the message reach its user.

For routine maintenance or status messages, a complete update matters more than a link that is fast only some of the time. The proposed route reuses suitable electrical wiring and ends at a dedicated light receiver. Extra equipment is still needed; mine coverage and cost savings have not been demonstrated.

THE CHALLENGE

The two links don’t keep the same pace.

Across 31 laboratory arrival records, PLC gaps reached about 5.46 seconds. A high mean delivery rate did not remove those pauses. Meanwhile, the optical link drains its queue at a limited rate, so both running empty and admitting too much data matter.

OUR SOLUTION

Store briefly. Send steadily.

A 105,472-byte FIFO stores the reserve. The selected mode starts at the declared 75% fill level and uses occupancy feedback to target 85%. In the integrated comparison it maintained headroom without recorded full-FIFO rejections. A finite reserve still cannot cover every measured interruption.

    How your score works

    Each attempt starts at 1,000 points. Subtract 20 points per percentage point of data unavailable at 60 seconds, and 100 points per second of peak FIFO queue delay. Scores stop at zero. Your ranked score is the rounded average of all three attempts. Your best complete round per nickname is ranked; earlier-started completed rounds break ties.

    Everyone uses the same deterministic traces. The server recalculates results from your buffer choices. Overflow and data still waiting at the deadline count toward missing image tiles. Queue delay is estimated from peak queued data ÷ optical drain rate; a larger empty buffer does not inherently slow the link.

    60 model seconds play in about 10 seconds on screen. Teaching rates: source 10 kB/s, PLC bursts 40 kB/s, optical drain 12.5 kB/s. These are not measured project rates. The game never controls the rig.

    PLC INVESTIGATION / REPORT UPDATED 26 OCTOBER 2026

    A fast link can still
    leave the user waiting.

    Broken delivery can interrupt a routine message even when average PLC speed is high. The investigation starts with those gaps, then asks how much data to hold in reserve and how to keep that reserve usable.

    HOW WE INVESTIGATED THE PROBLEM

    Measure first. Size the reserve. Compare the controls.

    Packet captures record when PLC data arrive. Their gaps inform the buffer requirement. A separate integrated comparison then tests five startup and sender-pacing modes with a charger connected, observing useful output and buffer fill.

    SELECTED TESTED CONFIGURATION

    Gated startup.
    Occupancy feedback.

    The source adjusts admission to preserve a reserve and leave room for arrivals. It does not change FIFO capacity or the optical clock.

    The selected mode delivered 201.24 kbit/s of received payload after the first 5 seconds and held 84.96% mean fill, with no recorded full-FIFO rejections across its three complete captures.

    Immediate fixed-rate buffering delivered slightly more payload, but saturated the queue. The selection favours inventory and admission headroom, rather than the highest output alone.

    What was compared?

    Five modes, with three 60-second repeats each. A 65 W laptop charger was connected, the iron was off, the optical path was 3 cm at 0-degree alignment, and the nominal optical setting was 280 kbit/s.

    The PLC characterisation used a TL-PA7017 pair. Integrated tests used TL-PA4010 and TL-WPA4220 adapters. Their advertised link rates are not application payload rates.

    Three different quantities: 300 kbit/s is the conservative sizing basis and tested optical maximum; 280 kbit/s is the integrated nominal setting; 201.24 kbit/s is the selected mode's measured received payload.

    Rejection counts cover three complete captures per mode, including startup. Received-payload and occupancy summaries exclude the first 5 seconds. Counter windows are observations at the polling resolution, not proof of gap-free output at every instant.

    Buffer sizing and evidence limits

    The 2.5 s target uses a 300 kbit/s drain assumption: 37.5 kB/s needs 93.75 kB for 2.5 seconds, within the 110 kB allowance. The implemented 105,472-byte FIFO holds about 2.11 seconds at 75% fill and 2.39 seconds at 85% fill on that assumed basis. Framing and actual transmission timing affect the running drain.

    The target comes from retrospective sizing over the 31 arrival records under ideal recovery. It is not independently validated outage coverage or an application-specific delay guarantee. Packet-arrival gaps are not one-way latency. No mine deployment, recorded-speech playback quality, mine-wide coverage or installation savings was demonstrated.

    Potential future use is non-critical maintenance or status delivery to a fixed optical receiving point, with site-specific cable, electrical, optical and equipment assessment still required.
